How to read the bars

Each bar is one geological stage, a slice of time a few million years long. The taller and brighter the bar, the bigger the share of sea-animal groups (genera, which are groups of closely related species) that disappeared during it. Scientists count groups rather than single species because fossils of whole groups survive better. For the Big Five, the labels also give the estimated share of species lost. The values are approximate and redrawn after Rohde & Muller (2005), who used Sepkoski's fossil compendium.

One scale, start to finish

Every centimetre of the graph stands for the same amount of time, from the birth of the Earth to today. That is why animals only appear in the last eighth of it, and why all of human history fits in its final 2 pixels. The recent extinctions are listed right after the graph for that reason.

What we can't count

Before about 540 million years ago, life was mostly microscopic and left few fossils, so nobody can put a percentage on those crises. The same goes for the ice age and recorded history, where we know individual species instead of fossil percentages. Those sections show events without bars.
